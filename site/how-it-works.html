How it works

Some background sounds come from events that public records log with an exact time and place. If you know when and where each event happened, you can work out where a microphone must have been to hear them when it did.

The idea: GPS in reverse

A GPS receiver works out where it is from the timing of signals sent by satellites at known positions. Earwitness does the same with sound. The recording is the receiver. The lightning flashes or planes are the satellites. Sound travels about 343 metres a second, so a clap from 3 km away arrives about 9 seconds after the flash.

The time the recording started is usually unknown, so Earwitness tries every start time. What pins down the place is the spacing between the sounds, not when they happened. Earwitness never tries to turn the audio into a location directly. It predicts what each candidate place would have heard and checks that against the recording.

Thunder

A weather satellite, NOAA's GOES Geostationary Lightning Mapper, records lightning flashes over the Americas, each with its time and position. For every candidate place on a grid, Earwitness works out when each nearby flash's thunder would have arrived, and how loud it would have been. It then slides that pattern along the recording's own loudness to find the best fit. The best-fitting places are refined on finer grids, down to 200 m.

  • Good case: a few distinct claps from flashes within about 10 km, on a day when there was little other lightning in the area searched.
  • Hard case: a big storm. The claps blur into one long rumble that fits many places. Distant thunder is just as hard: rumble from 10 to 25 km away looks a lot like rain or traffic.
  • Accuracy: a few kilometres at best. The satellite places each flash to about 3 to 4 km.

Propeller planes

As a small propeller plane flies past, the pitch of its drone slides down: higher while it comes closer, lower as it moves away. That is the Doppler effect. The shape of that slide shows how fast the plane was going and how close it came. It's usually too faint to hear, but it shows up clearly as a curved line on a spectrogram, a picture of the sound's pitch over time.

Most planes broadcast their position every second (ADS-B), and adsb.lol publishes those tracks for every day. Earwitness first narrows the search with simple checks: is there a plane with the right speed, passing at the right distance, at the right moment? It then computes the exact pitch curve each remaining place would have heard from the real flight track, and scores how well it lines up with the spectrogram.

  • Needs: two flyovers in the recording, or one flyover and the time the recording started. You mark each flyover in the app with its trace and fit tools.
  • Accuracy: about 100 to 200 m when it works.
  • Hard case: one flyover by a common training plane. Several look-alike planes nearby leave almost the same curve.

What you tell it

  • The recording: a local audio or video file.
  • The date it was made, or a range of dates (in UTC).
  • A rough region to search, such as a state or a metro area. Smaller is faster.
  • The start time, if you know it. It helps a lot.

What you get

  • A ranked list of likely places, with scores and the margin over the next best.
  • A heat map of how well each place fits.
  • For each answer, the predicted sound drawn over the recording's own, so you can see why it fits.

The margin over the runner-up matters more than the score itself. A close second place means the answer is uncertain.

Before you search: the audit

The first thing the app does is the audit: a quick check for which clues a recording contains at all. It looks for thunder, propeller flyovers, mains hum, and location or time tags in the file's metadata. It also tells you what each clue could reveal and what helps against it.
